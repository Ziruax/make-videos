# sk-1-folds — "The Five Folds"

`/make-sketchbook` example piece. 9:16, 36.9s @ 24fps, 120 BPM, motion on 2s,
voice-driven (Edge TTS + faster-whisper word times, 84/85 heard).

## Spec

- **The story:** a warm how-to — how to fold a paper plane that actually
  flies. One sketchbook page for the whole piece: the sheet is pencilled in
  and inked; five folds land one at a time (pencil → crease → flap → ink,
  the pencil prop following); **the secret** — one tiny fold at the nose —
  gets the page's single highlighter swipe and a small weight-vs-lift
  diagram; the wings go up into a shallow V; then **THE LAUNCH**: a shape
  morph where the folded dart *becomes* the flying plane, the payoff hit
  landing in the scored pause before "Throw it gently". The plane glides a
  hand-drawn path, loops, lands by the check mark, and the page says
  **go make one**.
- **Format:** one page, two eras, no hard cuts — the era boundary is the
  launch morph itself. The camera leans in as the folds land (z 1→1.14) and
  pulls back for the flight.
- **Style:** sketchbook. Graphite carries everything; ink settles it; the
  highlighter lands once (the nose); red underlines the tag and the final
  title; **terracotta is the maker-hero only**. Step numbers 1–6 in circled
  graphite. The page (dots, tape, smudges, the coffee ring) shows in every
  frame; an ant and margin stars keep it alive.

## Claims + sources

- Dihedral (wings up in a shallow V) stabilises the flight and prevents
  spiralling — foldnfly.com ("a small dihedral angle… more stable flight").
- Forward weight (a nose fold, a paperclip, copper tape at the front) puts
  the centre of gravity ahead of the centre of lift, which is what makes a
  plane glide instead of stall — physics.stackexchange.com (paperclip at
  ~quarter-chord); NYU applied-math study (copper tape on the front, 2022).
- "Throw it gently, almost level" is the standard advice on every paper-
  plane instruction site.
